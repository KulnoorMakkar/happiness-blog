The Myth of “I’ll Be Happy When I Get Something”

According to psychologists, the belief that happiness will come to us if we reach a certain goal in our life or accomplish something is called the “arrival fallacy.” Dr. Tal Ben-Shahar, a Harvard lecturer on positive psychology, explains that in most cases even if people do get what they wanted, the happiness that they get afterward is often temporary. In no time, it gets replaced by new pressures and bigger goals. Therefore, this creates an endless loop of chasing happiness, feeling empty afterward, and then chasing again.

This hit me when I finally got the visa to the country I’ve always dreamed of studying in. For months, I used to imagine that it would be the happiest moment of my life. It did stay the happiest moment of my life—but only for three to four days. Then I was stressed about assignments, finding friends, maintaining my GPA, and finding a part-time job. That joy eventually had to fade because I tied my happiness to a result rather than the journey itself.

Does Money Buy Happiness?

Another major myth that’s very common is that money equals happiness. A 2021 study by Daniel Kahneman and Matthew Killingsworth found that money can actually only increase happiness to a certain point—about $75,000/year for most people. Beyond that, emotional well-being doesn’t really improve much. It’s because we can never become emotionally fulfilled through just material things. It comes from relationships, purpose, and the right attitude toward life.

How we spend money also plays a major role in how it affects our happiness. Money spent on life-enriching experiences like traveling, investing in time-saving conveniences, or donating to others in need results in more lasting satisfaction than material purchases. Spiritually, true happiness comes from seeing and accepting life as it is, and just being grateful enough to experience every moment—good and bad.

What Actually Makes People Happy?

According to the longest-running study on happiness, the Harvard Study of Adult Development, the #1 predictor of lifelong happiness is close relationships, not career success, fame, or wealth. Practicing gratitude, affirmations, or journaling about what you’re thankful for has been proven to boost long-term mood.

Personally, the happiest moments in my life weren’t about awards or achievements. It was when I was in the flow state—the deep immersion where you lose track of time. Athletes feel it mid-play, artists during creation, and passionate programmers when locked into coding at 3 a.m. It’s not about validation. It’s just presence.

And What About Lotteries—The Shortcut to Dreams?

Lotteries sell people the fantasy of overnight success. But if you didn’t become someone through daily effort, you wouldn’t know how to handle the outcome. Many winners end up bankrupt, divorced, or depressed. We should promote financial literacy, mental health resources, and a culture that values meaning over money.

My Future Goals—and How I Plan to Stay Happy Along the Way

I still have big goals—I want to work in artificial intelligence, do research, and help others through tech. But I’m learning to enjoy the process. I practice affirmations, gratitude, and give myself breaks without guilt. I tell myself every day: My self-worth isn’t tied to a future version of me.

In the end, happiness isn’t about reaching a final destination. It’s about enjoying every moment—even setbacks. Real happiness isn’t always loud or dramatic. It’s subtle and calm. It’s waking up without dread, and loving without conditions.
